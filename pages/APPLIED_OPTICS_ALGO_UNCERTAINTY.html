4. Algorithmic Implementation and Uncertainty Analysis

Applied Optics draft section focused on relative transmittance inversion with spline-based thin-film reconstruction

The optical inversion is formulated as a staged spline-based estimation problem in which the film thickness, refractive-index dispersion, absorptive structure, and uncertainty corridors are inferred from the measured relative transmittance spectrum $T_{rel}(\lambda)$. The numerical design is intended to remain flexible enough to represent non-canonical spectral structures while preserving the geometric and physical constraints required for stable optimization.

4.1 Numerical framework and coordinate scaling

The inversion is not performed directly in linear wavelength coordinates. Over a broad spectral range, uniform sampling in $\lambda$ is poorly matched to the actual optical complexity of thin-film dispersion: the ultraviolet region usually contains the steepest refractive and absorptive variations, whereas the long-wavelength region is comparatively smoother. To better distribute model flexibility across the physically relevant structures, the computational domain is expressed in reciprocal wavelength,

$$ \sigma = \frac{1}{\lambda} \qquad (\mathrm{nm}^{-1}). $$

This transformation naturally increases the effective density of spline degrees of freedom in the high-energy region without requiring an excessive number of nodes over the entire spectrum. In practical terms, the optimization mesh is therefore allocated according to spectral complexity rather than according to the arbitrary spacing of the exported wavelength grid.

The extinction coefficient is handled through a second change of variables. Because a passive material must satisfy $k(\lambda) \ge 0$, direct optimization of $k$ would require explicit positivity constraints, which tend to create active-boundary effects and can degrade quasi-Newton updates. To avoid this issue, the absorption is parameterized by its logarithmic image,

$$ L = \ln k \qquad \Longleftrightarrow \qquad k = e^L. $$

In this representation, the optimizer acts on the unconstrained field $L(\sigma)$, whereas the forward optical model always receives a strictly non-negative extinction coefficient. Extremely weak absorption is thus represented smoothly through large negative values of $L$ rather than through discontinuous clipping of $k$ at zero. The pair $(\sigma, L)$ therefore defines a numerically stable coordinate system for broadband spline inversion.

4.2 Log-proportion spatial encoding

Once knot positions are allowed to move, a central numerical difficulty is to preserve their monotonic ordering. Direct optimization of the physical coordinates $\sigma_i$ can lead to node crossing, i.e., configurations for which $\sigma_{i+1} < \sigma_i$, which invalidates the interpolation geometry. To remove this failure mode at the parametrization level, the knot distribution is encoded through normalized positive proportions rather than through direct coordinates.

Let $\sigma_0$ and $\sigma_{K-1}$ denote the outermost knots. The interval between them is partitioned through positive weights $w_i$ satisfying $\sum_i w_i = 1$. The optimizer operates on unconstrained real variables $x_{enc,i}$, which are transformed into proportions through a Softmax-like mapping,

$$ w_i = \frac{\exp(x_{enc,i})}{\sum_{j=0}^{K-2} \exp(x_{enc,j})}, $$ $$ \sigma_i = \sigma_0 + (\sigma_{K-1}-\sigma_0)\sum_{j=0}^{i-1} w_j. $$

Because each $w_i$ is strictly positive, the decoded knot sequence remains strictly increasing for any optimizer step. This encoding preserves topological admissibility exactly, while still allowing the free-knot optimizer to concentrate nodes near abrupt spectral structures such as UV band-edge transitions or localized absorptive onsets.

The practical role of this encoding is not merely numerical convenience: it makes free-knot optimization feasible without imposing a large number of explicit inequality constraints on the optimizer.

4.3 Sequence-gated optimization pipeline

The inverse problem is highly correlated because thickness and refractive index can partially compensate each other through the optical phase product. For this reason, all degrees of freedom are not released simultaneously. Instead, the algorithm uses a sequence-gated pipeline in which progressively richer parameterizations are activated only after a lower-dimensional subproblem has been stabilized.

Step-by-step optimization sequence
  1. A deterministic Swanepoel-based initialization provides a first estimate of thickness and optical constants from extrema of $T_{rel}$.
  2. A rigid-knot stage (SOL 2) optimizes thickness and node amplitudes while keeping knot positions fixed.
  3. A free-knot stage (SOL 3) releases the geometric positions of the refractive and absorptive nodes so that the model can adapt its spatial resolution to local spectral complexity.
  4. A transparent-subband stabilization stage (SOL 3b) regularizes poorly observable absorptive degrees of freedom where $T_{rel}$ is weakly sensitive to $k$.
  5. A final spectral mesh polish re-optimizes the physical vector on the selected knot mesh using a smooth spline representation in $\sigma$.

Deterministic initialization

The initialization stage extracts spectral extrema from the measured relative transmittance and uses a Swanepoel-type construction to derive an initial refractive-index trend together with a first estimate of thickness. This step is essential because the inverse problem is phase-periodic: neighboring interference orders may generate visually plausible oscillations even when the thickness is incorrect. The initialization therefore acts as a basin-selection mechanism that places the later numerical optimization near the physically correct fringe family.

Rigid-topology stage (SOL 2)

In SOL 2, the knot positions are fixed and the unknown vector contains only thickness and node amplitudes,

$$ \mathbf{x}_{SOL2} = [d, n_1,\dots,n_K, L_1,\dots,L_K]. $$

A trust-bounded global search is first carried out around the initialized solution, after which a bounded L-BFGS-B polish refines the best incumbent. The purpose of this rigid stage is to identify the physically correct thickness before geometric adaptation of the mesh is allowed. By preventing knot migration at this stage, the algorithm forces the optimization to explain the observed fringes primarily through physically meaningful changes in thickness and node amplitudes rather than through premature re-topologization of the spline geometry.

Free-knot stage (SOL 3)

Once a coherent rigid solution has been obtained, the knot geometry is released. The refractive and absorptive meshes are treated independently, allowing the $n(\sigma)$ and $k(\sigma)$ representations to concentrate their degrees of freedom in different spectral regions. This is important because strong refractive curvature and strong absorption onset do not generally occur at the same wavelengths. The transition from SOL 2 to SOL 3 is performed through a consistent projection of the incumbent state into the split-knot parametrization, after which successive local passes with increasing optimization budgets refine the geometry and the node values.

Transparent-subband stabilization (SOL 3b)

In highly transparent regions, the relative transmittance becomes only weakly sensitive to absorption, so that the absorptive parameters are poorly identifiable from the data alone. In those regions, unconstrained free-knot optimization may generate spurious oscillations in $k(\lambda)$ that reflect numerical noise rather than measurable optical information. To suppress this artifact, the algorithm introduces a curvature penalty on the logarithmic absorption field,

$$ P_{reg} = W_{reg}\sum_i \left(L_{i+1} - 2L_i + L_{i-1}\right)^2. $$

This regularization is applied specifically where the sensitivity of $T_{rel}$ to $k$ becomes too weak to constrain the absorptive degrees of freedom robustly. It should therefore be interpreted as a stabilization of poorly observable modes rather than as an imposed material law.

Final spectral mesh polish

After the piecewise-linear exploratory stages, the final solution is re-optimized on the chosen mesh using a smooth spline representation in reciprocal wavelength. This last refinement removes residual staircase effects inherited from intermediate parameterizations and produces the final nominal curves used for reporting thickness, refractive index, extinction coefficient, and profiled uncertainty bounds.

4.4 Density-corrected objective and analytic Jacobian

The merit function compares the experimental relative transmittance $T_{rel,exp}(\lambda)$ to the transfer-matrix prediction $T_{rel,th}(\lambda; d,n,k)$. Because experimental spectra are commonly sampled on irregular wavelength grids, the residual sum cannot be interpreted as a physically fair broadband misfit unless the sampling density is corrected. For this reason, the objective function uses quadrature weights $w_{pix}(\lambda_m)$ defined on the masked wavelength grid,

$$ \mathrm{RMSE} = \sqrt{\frac{\sum_{m=1}^{N} w_{pix}(\lambda_m)\left[T_{rel,exp}(\lambda_m)-T_{rel,th}(\lambda_m;d,n,k)\right]^2}{\sum_{m=1}^{N} w_{pix}(\lambda_m)}}. $$

The weights are computed on the logarithmic wavelength axis through a trapezoidal quadrature rule. This construction makes the discrete objective approximate a continuous integral over the useful spectral domain and prevents oversampled wavelength pockets from dominating the fit merely because they contain more exported points.

A single objective call follows a fixed sequence. The current optimization vector is decoded into thickness and node parameters; the $n(\lambda)$ and $k(\lambda)$ fields are reconstructed on the active masked grid; the transfer-matrix model computes the theoretical relative transmittance; residuals with respect to the measured spectrum are formed; these residuals are weighted by the density-corrected quadrature coefficients; and any active regularization terms are added to the spectral misfit.

Local optimization is accelerated by analytic sensitivities of the optical model. Instead of relying exclusively on finite-difference perturbations, the implementation evaluates explicit derivatives of the transfer-matrix response with respect to thickness and spline parameters. This substantially improves efficiency and numerical robustness in the high-dimensional local stages, especially once free-knot relocation has been activated.

4.5 Profile likelihood and uncertainty corridors

The uncertainty analysis is based on profile likelihood with thickness as the profiled parameter. This choice is motivated by the strong coupling between thickness and the oscillatory structure of the relative transmittance spectrum. Rather than estimating uncertainty only from local curvature at the optimum, the algorithm explicitly measures how far the thickness can be displaced while the remaining spline degrees of freedom are allowed to re-accommodate the spectral fit.

Step-by-step construction of the uncertainty corridors
  1. The nominal optimum $d_{opt}$ and its associated spline solution are selected as the reference state.
  2. A family of trial thickness values is generated around $d_{opt}$.
  3. For each imposed thickness, $d$ is frozen and the remaining spline parameters are re-optimized locally.
  4. The resulting profiled objective values define the function $\chi^2(d)$, or equivalently the profiled RMSE curve.
  5. All solutions satisfying the likelihood-ratio acceptance rule are retained.
  6. The accepted family of solutions defines both the confidence interval on thickness and the wavelength-dependent uncertainty corridors for $n(\lambda)$ and $k(\lambda)$.
$$ \Delta\chi^2 = \chi^2(d) - \chi^2(d_{opt}) \le \chi^2_{1,0.95}. $$

The important point is that these corridors are not obtained from independent pointwise perturbations of optical constants. They are produced by families of compensating solutions that remain spectrally acceptable after a controlled perturbation of thickness. The resulting envelopes therefore reflect the actual coupling structure of the inverse problem and provide a more informative uncertainty description than local standard errors alone.

Because the profiled re-optimization is intentionally local rather than fully global, the resulting corridors should be interpreted as structured local uncertainty manifolds around the nominal optimum. This interpretation is particularly well suited to thin-film inversion, where the dominant uncertainty mechanism is often the partial compensation between thickness and dispersion rather than isolated pointwise noise on individual optical constants.
