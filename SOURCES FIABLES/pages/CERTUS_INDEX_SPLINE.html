CERTUS-INDEX-SPLINE

Scientific report on the spline-based index inversion pipeline

This document describes the mathematical structure, parameterization, optimization flow, and uncertainty analysis used by CERTUS-INDEX-SPLINE (Revision 2026.04.17).

Abstract
CERTUS-INDEX-SPLINE reconstructs the optical constants using a spline-based parameterization rather than a rigid oscillator model. The inverse problem is solved through a staged optimization pipeline that preserves numerical stability while allowing flexible reconstructions of $n(\lambda)$ and $k(\lambda)$. The method operates in reciprocal wavelength ($\sigma$) and uses a logarithmic absorption variable ($L = \ln k$) to preserve the positivity of $k$.

1. Motivation for a Model-Free Spline Representation

Classical thin-film models such as Lorentzian, Gaussian, Drude, and related oscillator forms encode strong physical assumptions about symmetry and band structure. These assumptions can be useful, but they can also be too restrictive for materials with complex absorption features.

Scientific Note (Causality vs. Expressivity): By moving away from classical parametric models (like TLU), the B-spline parameterization intentionally sacrifices rigorous Kramers-Kronig (KK) causality in favor of maximal empirical expressivity. The spline fits the observed data with high fidelity but does not strictly enforce the fundamental interdependent physics between $n$ and $k$.

In practice, materials such as non-stoichiometric oxynitrides, porous sol-gel matrices, or nanostructured transparent conducting oxides may not be well described by a small number of parametric oscillators. A spline-based representation allows the optical constants to follow the measured spectral curvature without imposing a rigid analytic shape.

Spline premise: CERTUS-INDEX-SPLINE uses floating spatial nodes so that the reconstructed optical functions can follow the measured transmission and reflection data with minimal analytic constraint.

Section 2: Fundamental Parameter Mapping Context

Optimizing free points across a spectrum necessitates an highly optimized physical coordinate scale to map against. Using purely linear wavelength ($\lambda$ in $\text{nm}$) spreads points uniformly across vast flat IR tails while grossly under-representing the extreme UV dynamics where the most violent structural index changes occur.

2.1 The Coordinate Proportional Inverse Map: $\sigma$

CERTUS natively operates uniformly inside the reciprocal wavenumber scaling domain: $$ \sigma = \frac{1}{\lambda} \quad (\text{units: } \text{nm}^{-1}) $$ By moving into $\sigma$-space, optimization arrays natively cluster in extreme density atop high-energy zones, mimicking physical reality where complex band-gap structures possess maximum derivative variances. This fundamentally prevents sparse node alignment from missing critical $k$ discontinuities.

2.2 Positivity Causality via Logarithmic Absorption ($L$)

A limitation of free-point arrays is that unconstrained gradients can drive the extinction coefficient $k$ below zero, which is not physically admissible for a passive substrate matrix. CERTUS avoids this issue by optimizing in logarithmic coordinates: $$ L = \ln(k) \quad \Rightarrow \quad k(\sigma) = \exp\left[L(\sigma)\right] $$ In this representation, negative updates in $L$ reduce $k$ smoothly toward zero without allowing sign changes, which makes the parameterization numerically stable for passive thin-film inversion.

Section 3: The CERTUS progression architecture Sequence Matrix

Simultaneously releasing tens of node pairs horizontally and vertically relative to a physical thickness variable $d$ constructs an immense correlation matrix. Specifically, variations in $d$ can instantly cancel out variations in $n(\sigma)$ to maintain optical thickness ($nd$ phase stability). If left fully unconstrained, any optimization algorithm falls instantly into localized dimensional "Runge" oscillations. CERTUS resolves this by utilizing rigorous, monotonic sequence-gating methodologies.

Section 4: Phase 01 Parameter Determinism (The Swanepoel Foundation)

An unconstrained matrix optimization demands an intelligent baseline; utilizing a random $n=1.5, k=0$ uniform flat-line risks stranding the descent gradient in catastrophic $2\pi$ phase skipping (aliasing). To construct starting variables that better mirror the physical structure, CERTUS implements a robust variant of the 1983 Swanepoel inversion method in spline_smart_init.py by mapping transmission extrema ($T_{max}$ and $T_{min}$).

Analytical Extraction of Pseudo Index ($N$) and Absolute Transmittance Scaling
By mapping the heavily filtered signal via scipy.signal.find_peaks with rolling convolution sizes proportionate to wavelength densities ($n_{pts}/120$), peak envelopes are localized. Standard spectrophotometric measurements often yield relative transmittance $T_{\text{measured}}(\lambda) = T(\lambda) / T_{\text{sub}}(\lambda)$. To convert these relative envelopes $T_M$ (upper) and $T_m$ (lower) into absolute transmittance, the bare substrate transmission formula is applied: $$ T_{\text{sub}}(\lambda) = \frac{2 n_s}{n_s^2 + 1} $$ $$ T_{M, \text{abs}}(\lambda) = T_M \times T_{\text{sub}}(\lambda) = T_M \times \frac{2 n_s}{n_s^2 + 1} $$ $$ T_{m, \text{abs}}(\lambda) = T_m \times T_{\text{sub}}(\lambda) = T_m \times \frac{2 n_s}{n_s^2 + 1} $$ Substituting these absolute envelopes into the classic Swanepoel equation: $$ N = 2 n_s \frac{T_{M, \text{abs}} - T_{m, \text{abs}}}{T_{M, \text{abs}} \cdot T_{m, \text{abs}}} + \frac{n_s^2 + 1}{2} $$ yields the following simplified relation implemented directly in the code (which operates on relative transmittance envelopes $T_M$ and $T_m$): $$ N(\lambda) = \frac{n_s^2 + 1}{2} \left[ 1 + 2 \frac{T_M - T_m}{T_M \cdot T_m} \right] $$ This directly permits the deduction of the thin film refractive component $n_{swan}$: $$ n_{swan} = \sqrt{ N + \sqrt{N^2 - n_s^2} } $$ Simultaneously, the theoretical transmittance formulas provide bounds for the theoretical base extinction variables mapping $k_{swan}$. This yields a strictly structured physical pre-initialization grid, dropping immediate L-BFGS-B phase vectors to near minimal error topologies prior to solver commencement.

4.1 Step-by-Step Role of the Smart Initialization

For publication purposes, the smart initialization should be understood as a deterministic anti-aliasing stage. The code does not treat it as a cosmetic warm start. It serves four algorithmic functions in sequence. First, it identifies a transparent-envelope structure from the raw transmission spectrum. Second, it converts these envelopes into a pseudo-index field. Third, it projects that field onto the canonical sigma-node mesh used by the later optimization phases. Fourth, it generates an initial thickness and corresponding $(n,k)$ state sufficiently close to the physically correct fringe family so that later local methods do not waste their degrees of freedom compensating an incorrect fringe order.

This design matters because the inverse problem is not merely nonlinear; it is phase-periodic. A thickness error of one or several interference orders can still produce visually plausible spectra if the refractive nodes are subsequently allowed to distort. The initialization stage therefore narrows the search to a physically meaningful basin before the high-dimensional optimizer is even allowed to act.

4.2 What Must Be Reported in a Paper About Initialization

A manuscript should state explicitly that CERTUS does not start from a flat optical-constant prior. It starts from a Swanepoel-derived estimate extracted from measured extrema, then re-sampled on the optimization mesh. This point is essential because the downstream convergence behavior, the probability of fringe-order mistakes, and the stability of the free-knot stages all depend strongly on that informed initialization. In comparative studies, any baseline method should therefore be distinguished according to whether it uses the same informed warm start or a generic initial guess.

Section 5: Phase 02 Vector Grids - The Rigid SOL 2 Architecture

Post-Phase 01 setup, the framework migrates into Phase 02 (SOL 2). Interference fringes possess severe harmonic redundancies: absolute physical depth ($d$) can slip into a neighboring fringe order without immediately inflating RMSE significantly if the nodes are completely uncoupled and capable of compensating instantly.

To isolate and finalize precisely the actual physical substrate depth, SOL 2 imposes absolute rigidity along the coordinate domain. The $\sigma$ positions are entirely mathematically frozen. The vector reduces to dimensions indexing solely vertical node variables coupled linearly with $d$: $$ \mathbf{x}_{SOL2} = \left[d_{nm}, \quad n_1, n_2 \dots n_K, \quad L_1, L_2 \dots L_K \right] \quad \Rightarrow \quad \dim(\mathbf{x}) = 1 + 2K $$

CERTUS delegates this space to PGlobalOptimizer. Instead of a single localized descent hook, Basin-Hopping mechanisms traverse trust regions bounded dynamically by the function _pglobal_bounds_trust_region. To prevent the algorithm from slipping into adjacent optical fringe interference orders, node interval constraints are manipulated inversely to spatial resolution.

As the meshing density $K$ expands, the tolerance variable $\rho$ contracts linearly between a loose bound ($\rho_{hi} = 0.5$) and a tight boundary ($\rho_{lo} = 0.12$). Furthermore, PGlobal enforces an asymmetrical regional relaxation explicitly: nodes resting geometrically beyond extreme spectrum bands ($\lambda \lt 600\text{ nm}$ or $\lambda \gt 3000\text{ nm}$) have their surrounding search boundaries artificially widened by a factor of 2.5x to compensate for the high signal uncertainty characteristic of extreme edge probe measurements (The Needle Probe).

5.1 Step-by-Step Description of SOL 2

The operational sequence of SOL 2 is as follows. A rigid state vector is assembled on the canonical knot mesh. A trust-bounded global exploration is then launched around the smart-initialized state rather than over the full admissible hypercube. The width of that trust region depends on mesh cardinality and is further relaxed at spectral edges where the initial estimate is less reliable. Once the best global observation has been found, CERTUS clips it back to the admissible bounds and immediately launches a local L-BFGS-B polish. The output of SOL 2 is therefore not simply a global-search result; it is a globally explored, locally polished, and incumbent-filtered rigid-knot solution.

5.2 Why SOL 2 Is Kept Rigid

The scientific logic of this rigidity is central. If horizontal knot motion were already allowed at this stage, the solver could trade thickness error against geometric relocation of refractive and absorptive features. By freezing knot positions, SOL 2 forces the optimization to spend most of its flexibility on the vertical optical response rather than on re-topologizing the spectrum. In practical terms, Phase 02 is a thickness-locking phase before it is an optical-constant-refinement phase.

5.3 Publication-Relevant Output of SOL 2

What emerges from SOL 2 is the first internally coherent estimate of thickness, node amplitudes, and displayable $n(\lambda), k(\lambda)$ curves on the active objective domain. This state also becomes the reference warm start for the later free-knot transitions. Therefore, when describing the method in an article, SOL 2 should be presented as the stage that transforms a Swanepoel-informed prior into the first optimizer-consistent physical estimate.

Section 6: Phase 03 - Free-Knot Dimension Splitting (SOL 3)

Fixed-knot frameworks fall catastrophically short when mapping edge transitions (band-gaps). A structural UV band-edge discontinuity demands an extremely high spatial concentration of points to render accurate curvature, whereas visible regimes require near none. In Phase 03, Spatial constraints unlock entirely.

6.1 Mechanical Decoupling ($4K - 1$ Variables)

Because refractive dispersion changes occur dynamically differently than absorption thresholds, identical spatial grid constraints are physically inefficient. Phase 03 splits $\sigma$ distributions: $\sigma_n$ arrays drift independently from $\sigma_L$ structures.

6.2 Topological Preservation Mapping (Log-Proportion Encoding)

An unconstrained horizontal descent engine will invariably attempt to slide Node 3 spatially behind Node 2 ($\sigma_3 \lt \sigma_2$), generating instant mathematical implosions within array handlers expecting monotonic sequences. Rather than slowing the optimizer down with billions of complex local bound inequalities, the system implements robust log-proportion scalar encoding maps (Log-Softmax) embedded directly inside the solver matrix wrappers inside spline_objective.py:

The Log-Proportion Spatial Encoder and Monotonicity Enforcement
A knot vector $\sigma$ is encoded by the relative distances between consecutive knots. For $K$ nodes, we define $K-1$ weights $w_i = (\sigma_{i+1} - \sigma_i) / (\sigma_{K-1} - \sigma_0)$. The optimizer operates on $x_{enc, i} = \ln(w_i)$ which are unconstrained in $(-\infty, \infty)$. The decoding back to physical $\sigma$-coordinates follows: $$ w_i = \frac{\exp(x_{enc, i})}{\sum_{j=0}^{K-2} \exp(x_{enc, j})} $$ $$ \sigma_i = \sigma_0 + (\sigma_{K-1} - \sigma_0) \sum_{j=0}^{i-1} w_j $$ Consequently, no matter exactly how aggressive the L-BFGS-B gradient vector commands a displacement modification, $\exp(x_{enc, i})$ is strictly positive, assuring sequence order preservation. To guarantee numerical stability and prevent division by zero or node merging under extreme updates, the decoder enforces a relative minimum separation constraint: $$ \epsilon_s = \max\left(10^{-10},\,\, \text{SIGMA\_KNOTS\_MIN\_SEP\_REL} \times (\sigma_{K-1} - \sigma_0)\right) $$ where $\text{SIGMA\_KNOTS\_MIN\_SEP\_REL} = 0.005$ by default. Knots are then clipped sequentially: $$ \sigma_i = \max\left(\sigma_i,\,\, \sigma_{i-1} + \epsilon_s\right) \quad \text{for } 1 \le i \le K-1 $$

6.3 Warm-Start Projection from SOL 2 into SOL 3

The transition from SOL 2 to SOL 3 is not a blind restart. CERTUS projects the incumbent rigid solution onto the split-knot parameterization and checks that the projected SOL 3 initialization remains spectrally aligned with the incoming SOL 2 state. This safeguard changes the coordinate system without changing the physical model represented by the warm start.

6.4 Three Internal Local Phases: Descent, Polish, Deep Polish

Once in the free-knot domain, CERTUS does not run a single undifferentiated local solve. The code executes a sequence of progressively more expensive bounded L-BFGS-B phases internally labeled descent, polish, and, for the full SOL 3 branch, deep polish. Each phase starts from the best incumbent of the previous phase, uses a larger function-evaluation budget, and keeps tracking the best spectral objective value encountered. This phase-within-phase structure is scientifically useful because the early solve captures the bulk relocation of topology, whereas later solves sharpen the optimum once the search has entered the correct geometric basin.

6.5 Why the Best Seen Spectral State Is Preserved

The free-knot stages optimize a highly coupled spectral objective. Because termination may occur by budget exhaustion, line-search saturation, or simply because the last quasi-Newton step is not the best one seen, the code stores the best spectral incumbent throughout the trajectory. For a paper, this means the local solution should be described as the best observed incumbent over the bounded L-BFGS-B trajectory, not merely the terminal solver state.

Section 7: Phase 04 - Isolating the Transparent Plane (SOL 3b)

Deep into highly transparent regions mapping (e.g. Glass Substrates in VIS-NIR domain), explicit calculated values of $k$ plummet exponentially downwards (e.g. $10^{-6}$). Analytically, the derivative gradient of global Transmittance concerning extremely low $k$ variations approaches mathematical absolute zero: $$ \frac{\partial T(\lambda)}{\partial L_{transparent}} \approx 0 $$

In the absence of constraints, nodes in low-sensitivity regions can drift under numerical noise. In SOL 3, transparent $\sigma_L$ nodes may therefore become weakly identified if they are left fully free.

Phase 04 (SOL 3b) resolves this structurally. The $n(\sigma)$ nodes are frozen, and the vector is reduced to the absorptive variables $L_i$ and $\sigma_L$. A continuous penalty is applied to the second derivative of the absorptive profile: $$ P_{reg} = W_{reg} \cdot \sum \left( L_{i+1} - 2L_i + L_{i-1} \right)^2 $$ This penalizes oscillatory behavior and keeps low-sensitivity sub-arrays smooth and physically stable.

Scientific Note (Tikhonov Bias): Tikhonov regularization intrinsically introduces a bias-variance trade-off. Penalizing curvature mathematically introduces a deliberate smoothing bias, meaning it suppresses noise but may also artificially broaden true, narrow physical absorption peaks.

7.1 Step-by-Step Role of SOL 3b

SOL 3b is a sensitivity-conditioned sub-problem. The incoming state from the broader optimization is projected onto a representation in which the refractive structure is fixed. The algorithm then optimizes only the absorptive geometry and amplitudes in regions where the transmission sensitivity to $k$ would otherwise be too weak to constrain them robustly. If the projected warm start is already worse than the incoming incumbent by a meaningful margin, the code can skip the stage rather than force a degrading refinement.

Section 8: Objective Function Mathematics

8.1 Global Weighted Error Arrays

The centralized error computation handles overlapping photometric sources inside SplinePWLObjective:

$$ \text{MSE}_{Full} = \frac{W_T \sum_{m} \left( T_{obs}(\lambda_m) - T_{calc}(d, n, k, n_s) \right)^2 \cdot w_{pix}(\lambda_m) + W_R \sum_{m} \left( R_{obs}(\lambda_m) - R_{calc} \right)^2 \cdot w_{pix}}{W_T + W_R} $$

To compute $T_{calc}$ and $R_{calc}$, CERTUS employs the Transfer Matrix Method (TMM) incorporating an incoherent thick substrate backing. The coherent thin-film is evaluated via the characteristic matrix product: $$ \mathbf{M}_{film} = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\delta & \frac{i}{y_{film}}\sin\delta \\ i \cdot y_{film} \sin\delta & \cos\delta \end{pmatrix} \quad \text{where} \quad \delta = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda}(n-ik)d $$ The interface intensity responses $R_{12}, T_{12}$ are collected, and subsequently combined with the incoherent internal reflections mapping the macroscopic thick glass volume ($n_s$): $$ T_{calc} = \frac{T_{12} T_{23}}{1 - R_{21} R_{23}} \quad ; \quad R_{calc} = R_{12} + \frac{T_{12} T_{21} R_{23}}{1 - R_{21} R_{23}} $$

The spatial weighting coefficient $w_{pix}(\lambda_m)$ represents trapezoidal density integrals computed from the grid spacing. If a spectrophotometer takes 1500 points across 10 nm in a cross-over region but only 10 points per 10 nm elsewhere, $w_{pix}$ normalizes the variance contribution and prevents oversampled regions from dominating the descent.

8.2 Why the Transmission Weights Are Essential When the Wavelength Grid Is Arbitrary

In real spectrophotometric workflows, the wavelength grid is rarely uniform. Instruments may concatenate detector ranges, densify sampling near cut-overs, or export acquisition vectors with arbitrary step sizes. If one minimizes a naive unweighted sum $\sum_m (T_{obs}-T_{calc})^2$, the optimization reflects the most densely sampled regions rather than the broadband transmission discrepancy.

CERTUS explicitly avoids that bias. The transmission channel is not weighted by a heuristic visual preference, but by a quadrature rule that approximates a continuous spectral integral over the domain actually seen by the optimizer. The practical consequence is straightforward: doubling the point density in a narrow wavelength pocket must not double that pocket's physical authority over the fit if the additional samples carry no new spectral span. The role of the per-pixel coefficient $w_{pix}(\lambda_m)$ is precisely to restore this invariance.

Continuous Integral Approximation and Quadrature Scheme
To prevent non-uniform grid densities from biasing the fit, the objective function approximates the continuous integrated discrepancy over the logarithmic wavelength spectrum: $$ \int_{\ln \lambda_{\min}}^{\ln \lambda_{\max}} \left[ T_{\text{obs}}(\lambda) - T_{\text{calc}}(\lambda) \right]^2 \mathrm{d}(\ln \lambda) \approx \sum_{m=1}^{N} \left[ T_{\text{obs}}(\lambda_m) - T_{\text{calc}}(\lambda_m) \right]^2 w_m $$ where the unnormalized trapezoidal quadrature weights $w_m$ are computed using a central-difference stencil on the logarithmic coordinate $u_m = \ln(\lambda_m)$: $$ w_1 = |u_2 - u_1| $$ $$ w_N = |u_N - u_{N-1}| $$ $$ w_m = \frac{1}{2} |u_{m+1} - u_{m-1}| \quad \text{for } 2 \le m \le N-1 $$ To keep the final Root-Mean-Square Error (RMSE) numerically consistent and comparable to a traditional pointwise sample standard deviation, the weights are renormalized to sum to the number of active points $N$: $$ \tilde{w}_m = w_m \cdot \frac{N}{\sum_{j=1}^N w_j} $$ The normalized weights $\tilde{w}_m$ correspond to the $w_{pix}(\lambda_m)$ terms utilized directly in the objective calculation.

The choice of the logarithmic wavelength axis is deliberate. Thin-film spectra are not physically homogeneous in linear $\lambda$: a 10 nm interval near 300 nm does not represent the same optical scale as a 10 nm interval near 3000 nm. By integrating on $\ln(\lambda)$, CERTUS gives each relative spectral decade a fairer contribution. This is aligned with the solver's broader philosophy: the optical content of the UV-VIS region is structurally denser, while the IR extends over very large absolute wavelength spans. A pure linear-$\lambda$ weighting would over-privilege the long-wave tail simply because it occupies more nanometers.

Equally important, these are not user-defined artistic weights on transmission. The global scalar $W_T$ still controls the balance between the transmission and reflection channels, but inside the transmission term itself the factor $w_{pix}(\lambda_m)$ is a sampling-compensation weight. Its job is numerical fairness, not physical reinterpretation of the data. In other words:

Interpretation Principle: $W_T$ answers the question "How much should transmission matter relative to reflection?" whereas $w_{pix}(\lambda)$ answers the entirely different question "Given the actual exported wavelength grid, what is the physically fair contribution of this sample to the broadband misfit?" Confusing these two roles would lead to distorted fits and misleading residual priorities.

This distinction becomes decisive when the grid is irregular. Suppose an instrument outputs 500 extra points around a detector stitching zone. A naive optimizer would be tempted to spend a disproportionate amount of freedom in $n(\lambda)$, $k(\lambda)$, and even thickness $d$ merely to shave residuals in that tiny neighborhood, because those residuals appear hundreds of extra times in the sum. CERTUS's quadrature weights collapse those redundant samples back to approximately the spectral influence corresponding to their true wavelength span. The solver therefore chases a fit that is broadband-optimal, not export-format-optimal.

Another major advantage is robustness to ascending or descending grids. The implementation takes the absolute value of the trapezoidal increments, so the effective spectral contribution is identical whether the data arrive ordered from UV to IR or from IR to UV. This makes the optimizer invariant to acquisition/export conventions while keeping the exact same physical meaning for the objective.

In summary, CERTUS chooses the transmission weights so that the discrete objective behaves like a continuous integral over the useful spectrum, not like a raw count of file rows. This is indispensable precisely because the wavelength grid is arbitrary. Without this correction, any irregular acquisition density would silently rewrite the optimization problem itself.

8.3 The Semantic Definition of "Analytic Gradient"

It is imperative for scientific readers to clearly distinguish CERTUS's internal nomenclature. In many legacy optical engines, "analytic" referred to closed-form phenomenological dispersion laws (e.g. Tauc-Lorentz, Cody-Lorentz, or Cauchy families). CERTUS-INDEX-SPLINE uses the term differently: here it refers to an analytic derivative of the objective, not to a closed-form constitutive model for $n(\lambda)$ or $k(\lambda)$.

When CERTUS logs mention compute_spline_pwl_objective_analytic_gradient, "analytic" refers exclusively to the way the gradient is evaluated. Rather than relying on repeated finite-difference perturbations of the full parameter vector, the engine computes the derivative of the optical objective through dedicated sensitivity kernels. The resulting gradient provides direct derivatives with respect to thickness and spline parameters, improving numerical efficiency and reducing finite-difference noise in the local bounded L-BFGS-B phases.

8.4 Step-by-Step Evaluation of a Single Objective Call

One objective evaluation in CERTUS proceeds in a fixed sequence. The current optimization vector is decoded into physical thickness and node variables. These variables are interpolated onto the masked wavelength grid. The transfer-matrix model computes synthetic transmission and, when applicable, reflection. The residuals against the masked experimental data are formed. Each residual is multiplied by the density-corrected quadrature weight derived from the wavelength grid. The transmission and reflection channels are then combined through the user-level channel weights $W_T$ and $W_R$. Finally, any stage-specific penalties are added if the current phase is not configured as pure spectral. This explicit order is useful in a paper because it separates optical forward modeling, spectral quadrature, and regularized inverse fitting as three conceptually distinct layers.

Section 9: Physical Barrier Constraints

Splines do not inherently enforce optical physics laws. Physical plausibility must therefore be introduced through external penalties and staged constraints.

9.1 Anomalous Normal Dispersion Barrier Penalties

Under normal conditions and far from resonant electronic transitions, dielectric media typically follow the Cauchy relation $n(\lambda) = A + B/\lambda^2$, which implies a monotonic decrease of $n$ with wavelength. Unconstrained free-node vectors may invert that trend to absorb noise.

CERTUS uses n_lambda_rising_with_wavelength_penalty as a soft barrier localized to the selected optical zones. For $\Delta n = n(\sigma_j) - n(\sigma_{j+1})$ with $\sigma_j < \sigma_{j+1}$: $$ P_{anomaly} = W_{disp} \cdot \sum_{j} \left[ \max\left(0, \,\, \Delta n_{j} - \text{slack}_\epsilon\right) \right]^2 $$ The slack term is small ($\text{slack}_\epsilon \approx 0.001$) and preserves L-BFGS-B stability.

9.2 The K-Floor Clamping Vector

Instrument limits require lower bounds on absorption. Pure $0$ absorption would produce unphysical transmission scaling. During constraint finalization, enforce_k_floor_on_nodes applies a global floor across $L(\sigma)$. When optimizer variables fall below $1 \cdot 10^{-5}$, the code inserts values at the limit plane instead of clipping in place, which avoids interpolation distortion.

9.3 Why These Are Called Soft Barriers Rather Than Hard Constraints

CERTUS does not solve a fully constrained symbolic problem at every iterate. It augments the spectral objective with continuous penalties that discourage physically implausible behavior while preserving a smooth landscape for quasi-Newton updates. These terms should therefore be described as soft regularizing barriers whose strength depends on the optimization stage.

Section 10: IR Mesh Extension

Standard logarithmic grids (K=12) may fail to resolve structural resonances in the far-IR region. CERTUS therefore uses a topology extension when the spectral range requires it.

If $\lambda_{max} > 4000 \text{ nm}$, the mesh expands to 14 nodes. The additional indices are inserted with _insert_two_equi_lambda_in_last_sigma_segment, which splits the long-wavelength segment into three equal sub-intervals.

In this framework, K=12 is the compact topology for UV-VIS-NIR spectra, while K=14 is the far-IR extension used when the measured window genuinely reaches the long-wavelength regime. The additional nodes are concentrated in the final segment to recover far-IR flexibility without reducing the UV-VIS resolution already provided by K=12.

10.1 Publication Interpretation of the IR Extension

This extension should be described as an adaptive model-order rule tied to spectral support. The parameterization depends on whether the measured spectrum contains enough far-infrared information to justify additional degrees of freedom. Reporting should therefore include both the nominal node family and the spectral coverage that triggered the chosen topology.

In a manuscript, the practical rule is straightforward: if the fitted spectrum remains below the far-IR trigger, report the 12-node canonical topology; if the spectral support extends beyond the trigger and the last sigma segment is subdivided, report the 14-node IR-extended topology. This distinction changes both the dimension of the inverse problem and the class of long-wave absorption and dispersion shapes that the model can represent.

Section 12: Phase 09 Corridor Engine

The corridor stage implemented in spline_profile_corridors.py is not a generic post-processing utility. It is a dedicated profiled-refit engine designed to quantify local identifiability of thickness $d$ while preserving the same spectral forward model and masking conventions as the main spline inversion pipeline.

In practical terms, CERTUS does not treat uncertainty as a single scalar around $d_{opt}$. It constructs one or more iterative profiled processes in $d$: a continuation acceptance scan (threshold-based walks from $d_{\mathrm{opt}}$) and/or a regular diagnostic grid with optional breakpoint-driven extras, a post-sweep P0 rescue pass, and explicit logging when a new absolute-best refit RMSE is found. In all cases it freezes $d$, re-optimizes spline node variables locally on the same spectral mask, then aggregates accepted or sampled states into thickness intervals and envelopes for $n(\lambda)$ and $k(\lambda)$.

The codebase distinguishes two complementary corridor objects that should not be conflated in manuscripts: (i) the acceptance corridor built by profiled continuation logic (compute_profiled_corridors_by_d) and (ii) the regular-grid RMSE(d) diagnostic corridor built by forced $\Delta d$ sampling (compute_regular_grid_rmse_profile). The first defines accepted uncertainty envelopes; the second provides dense local geometry diagnostics and reproducibility checks.

12.1 Base Snapshot and Profile Seed Consistency

Both corridor branches build an effective base snapshot when a polished nominal pack exists (extract_nominal_best_polished_corridor_reference), so displayed $n(\lambda),k(\lambda)$, knots, and optional packed mesh state align with the reference used for $\mathrm{RMSE}_{\mathrm{ref}}$ in scientific nominal mode. The regular-grid worker then extracts mesh-consistent node vectors from that snapshot (or falls back to the passed result dict).

The code then extracts mesh-consistent node vectors and reconstructs a nodes-only initial vector for fixed-$d$ refits. If the mesh-packed state is unavailable, the code rebuilds a consistent nodes vector and clips it to bounds before profiling.

12.2 Fixed-$d$ Refit Objective Actually Used

During each profile point, CERTUS minimizes a masked spectral objective at frozen thickness using SplinePWLObjective with local L-BFGS-B. For corridor profiling, the implementation explicitly supports pure spectral mode so that the local refit is not dominated by regularization artifacts.

In the fixed-$d$ subproblem, the solver optimizes only node variables (refractive slice + absorptive slice). The reported profile RMSE is recomputed from the resulting $n(\lambda),k(\lambda)$ on the same masked spectral domain. If this recomputation becomes numerically non-finite, the code falls back to $\sqrt{\max(\text{MSE}_{objective},0)}$ to preserve point availability.

12.3 Profiled acceptance corridor (continuation in $d$)

Separate from the diagnostic grid, compute_profiled_corridors_by_d builds the acceptance corridor: a continuation-style scan around the nominal thickness $d_{\mathrm{opt}}$ using the same fixed-$d$ refit engine (_best_fit_at_d, multi-start with jitter, bounded L-BFGS-B). Each accepted point satisfies a user-chosen rule linking its refit RMSE to a reference RMSE on the same spectral mask (heuristic scale factor times $\mathrm{RMSE}_{\mathrm{ref}}$, fixed $\mathrm{RMSE}_{\mathrm{ref}}+\Delta$, adaptive $\Delta$ from a local quadratic estimate of $\mathrm{RMSE}(d)$ plus roughness, or LR / $\Delta\chi^2$ mode when enabled).

Scientific nominal mode (when enabled): $\mathrm{RMSE}_{\mathrm{ref}}$ is tied to spectral_rmse_best_value on the polished nominal $n(\lambda),k(\lambda)$; the nominal curves are included as the first family member without widening the envelope toward a separate solver export. After that, the pipeline runs a center refit at $d_{\mathrm{opt}}$ as a best-of-$N$ multi-start (label Center in logs). With adaptive absolute tolerance, auxiliary probes (AdaptiveTol ±i) sample nearby thicknesses to stabilize an effective $\Delta$ before the walks.

Default absolute slack (code defaults, 2026.05.08): for rmse_threshold_mode = abs_delta_adaptive (INDEX-SPLINE default), the nominal additive slack rmse_abs_tolerance / corridor_profile_d_rmse_abs_tolerance is $2.5\times 10^{-4}$ on the masked spectral RMSE scale. The adaptive floor adaptive_rmse_abs_min is $2.5\times 10^{-5}$. Crucially, the algorithm determines the active tolerance $\Delta_{\mathrm{eff}}$ dynamically by fitting a local quadratic $\text{RMSE}(d)$ valley using a probing step of 0.5 nm. It then multiplies the residual noise (MAD) of this parabola by a noise factor of 2.0. This physically meaningful "Profile Likelihood" approach ties the corridor width directly to the physical rigidity of the index/thickness coupling and the experimental noise floor, rejecting arbitrary hardcoded thresholds.

Parabolic Approximation & Robust Noise Estimation: $$ \text{RMSE}_{\text{fit}}(d) = c_2 (d - d_0)^2 + c_1 (d - d_0) + c_0 $$ $$ r_d = \text{RMSE}_{\text{obs}}(d) - \text{RMSE}_{\text{fit}}(d) $$ $$ \text{MAD} = \text{median} \left| r_d - \text{median}(r_d) \right| $$ $$ \sigma_{\text{prof}} = 1.4826 \times \text{MAD} $$ $$ \Delta_{\text{eff}} = \max(2.0 \times \sigma_{\text{prof}},\, \Delta_{\min}) $$
Envelope Span Bounds: $$ \Delta n(\lambda) = \max_{d \in D_{\text{accept}}} n_{\text{sol}}(\lambda, d) - \min_{d \in D_{\text{accept}}} n_{\text{sol}}(\lambda, d) $$ $$ \Delta k(\lambda) = \max_{d \in D_{\text{accept}}} k_{\text{sol}}(\lambda, d) - \min_{d \in D_{\text{accept}}} k_{\text{sol}}(\lambda, d) $$ $$ \text{with } D_{\text{accept}} = \{ d \mid \text{RMSE}(d) \le \text{RMSE}_{\text{ref}} + \Delta_{\text{eff}} \} $$

Walk phase: two thickness marches proceed from the center seed, typically in parallel ($+d$ and $-d$, configurable). Each step applies the same fixed-$d$ refit budget; continuation may auto-extend the span when the RMSE slope toward the threshold implies a longer excursion. When a trial crosses the active threshold, the code can bisection-refine the admissible boundary. The outcome is a reported $d$ interval plus $n(\lambda),k(\lambda)$ envelopes over accepted refits.

12.4 Regular-grid RMSE($d$): visit order, breakpoints, reverse exploration, P0, global-opt

The diagnostic path compute_regular_grid_rmse_profile walks a user-defined list of thicknesses (uniform step grid or equivalent). Points are visited from a grid anchor center outward in alternating right/left order for warm-start continuity. The anchor is the requested grid center passed from the GUI (visit_anchor_nm, typically the $d^\ast$ chosen in the RMSE tab), not necessarily the nominal-pack thickness used elsewhere for seed consistency—so a new user recentering drives the visit order and the first central refit location on the grid.

In addition to base grid points, the engine can inject extra points when a breakpoint is detected on one side walk.

Breakpoint detection is not one-rule-only. The code combines:

  • Legacy abrupt-gain trigger (absolute and relative RMSE gain thresholds),
  • Prev-N trigger (improvement against the best recent side history),
  • Parabolic trigger (gain against local quadratic prediction).

When triggered, CERTUS logs the breakpoint; branch sense can be chosen via a short best-of-two probe on either side of the break before launching reverse-direction exploration along $d$. It then evaluates a global-opt polish from the break seed (quick_pwlnk_refit_result_dict in code). Each such evaluation is compared to the running stored global-best candidate under the corridor “beat absolute” rule; the GUI may later merge that candidate into the nominal dict when it improves the exported optimum. Whenever a new absolute best RMSE is established along this entire diagnostic run (grid point, duplicate replacement, global-opt evaluation, or post-pass), the logger emits an explicit ABSOLUTE BEST (new record) line with full-precision d_nm and RMSE and a short tag (origin of the record).

After the main sweep, a P0 re-pass rescues “suspect” points whose RMSE sits abnormally above their thickness neighbours (local plateau heuristic): each suspect is re-fitted once using the node seed from the best point on the curve so far, which can break propagated local minima from chained warm starts.

12.5 Robustness Layers Added for Full $\Delta d$ Coverage

The current implementation contains explicit robustness layers to avoid missing requested grid points:

  • Fallback seed evaluation if local refit fails at a given $d$.
  • Fallback objective RMSE if spectral RMSE recomputation is non-finite.
  • Emergency recovery pass to fill still-missing requested base-grid points.
  • Final coverage audit reporting requested vs returned base points and missing-after-emergency count.

This means the corridor output now encodes both value and provenance of each RMSE(d) point, rather than silently discarding difficult points.

Importantly, this robustness stack is intentionally asymmetric: profile points produced by standard local refit remain the preferred statistical evidence, while fallback points are preserved for continuity, diagnostics, and explicit traceability rather than hidden interpolation.

12.6 Point Provenance and Diagnostics

For the regular-grid profile, each point carries a status code in profile_d_manual_grid_point_status_code:

  • 0: standard local refit point,
  • 1: fallback-from-seed point,
  • 2: fallback-from-objective point,
  • 3: emergency recovered point.

Aggregate diagnostics are also returned, including base/extra point counts and fallback counters. This allows publication-grade reporting of corridor reliability, not only corridor width.

In addition, the regular-grid worker now returns explicit coverage invariants (requested_base_points, returned_base_points, missing_after_emergency, coverage_complete). These quantities should be reported when corridor reproducibility is critical.

12.7 Interpreting RMSE(d): Best Sampled Point, Parabolic Center, Symmetric Display

The GUI and export logic intentionally distinguish three different notions:

  • Best sampled point (discrete grid minimum),
  • Local parabolic center (continuous local estimate when valley is sufficiently regular),
  • Displayed symmetric corridor (visualized around the chosen center for readability).

The authoritative uncertainty evidence remains the set of sampled profiled refits; the parabola is a local geometric estimator, not a replacement acceptance rule.

12.8 Why This Stage Is Local Sensitivity, Not Full Re-Inversion

The corridor engine is intentionally local and budget-constrained at each fixed-$d$ point. Its scientific role is to map how much the optical state can re-accommodate controlled thickness perturbations while preserving spectral quality, not to solve a new unconstrained global inverse problem at every tested thickness.

Therefore, corridor outputs should be interpreted as profiled local compensation manifolds around a chosen nominal state. They quantify conditional robustness of $(d,n,k)$ under constrained perturbation, not globally unique material truth.

Operational Interpretation: Corridor outputs should be reported as a structured local uncertainty object: sampled RMSE(d) valley, acceptance-derived interval(s), and corresponding $n(\lambda),k(\lambda)$ envelopes, with explicit point provenance diagnostics.

Section 13: Implementation Invariants for Reproducibility

For a publication intended to be reproducible, it is not sufficient to describe CERTUS conceptually. Several concrete implementation invariants materially affect the returned solution and therefore must be stated explicitly in any Methods section.

13.1 Objective Masking and Effective Spectral Domain

The optimization is never performed on an abstract full grid by default. CERTUS first constructs an objective mask that removes non-finite entries and optionally restricts the fit to a user-selected wavelength interval $[\lambda_{lo}, \lambda_{hi}]$ through rmse_fit_lambda_nm. All subsequent objective quantities—masked wavelength vector, reciprocal coordinates, substrate index vector, experimental $T/R$ arrays, and density-corrected weights—are built on this reduced domain. Consequently, every quoted RMSE must be interpreted relative to the active masked grid, not merely the imported file.

13.2 State Vector Semantics

The core solver carries two different state-vector families. In rigid SOL 2, the unknown vector is: $$ x_{SOL2} = [d, n_1, \dots, n_K, L_1, \dots, L_K] $$ while in the split free-knot regime the dimensionality expands to independent node-position and node-value blocks. This distinction is important because the optimizer is not simply refining values on a fixed physical mesh: in SOL 3 the code manipulates a geometric encoding of the mesh itself. The manuscript should therefore distinguish clearly between value refinement and topology refinement.

13.3 Canonical Knot Cardinality and IR Extension

The canonical number of sigma nodes is not universal. The default topology uses 12 nodes, but the code automatically expands to 14 nodes when $\lambda_{max} > 4000\,\text{nm}$. This rule is not cosmetic; it changes the dimension of the optimization landscape, the knot spacing in the far IR, and therefore the attainable refractive/absorptive curvature. Any benchmark or comparison study should report whether the 12-node or 14-node canonical topology was active.

More specifically, K=12 should be understood as the baseline compact topology for standard spectral spans, whereas K=14 is a targeted IR augmentation that increases expressivity only where the spectrum justifies it. Reporting merely that a "spline model" was used is therefore insufficient: the chosen knot cardinality is part of the physical model class actually fitted to the data.

13.4 Spectral-Only vs Penalized Objectives

A central reproducibility point is that CERTUS does not use one single objective for every stage. The standard extraction phases may include soft physical penalties such as the normal-dispersion barrier, whereas the corridor-profiling stages can switch to a pure spectral objective. This staged deactivation is deliberate: it prevents uncertainty intervals from collapsing into regularization artifacts. In a paper, these phases should be presented as distinct estimation regimes rather than as one monolithic minimization.

13.5 Best-Incumbent Retention Instead of Blindly Trusting the Last Iterate

Throughout the local L-BFGS-B phases, CERTUS explicitly keeps track of the best objective value seen during the run. This matters because the final iterate returned by a bounded quasi-Newton routine can be slightly worse than the best incumbent when termination occurs by budget exhaustion or by final line-search behavior. The code therefore preserves the best-seen solution rather than blindly accepting the last solver state. From a publication perspective, this means the algorithm should be described as an incumbent-tracking optimizer, not merely a direct wrapper around the raw terminal state of SciPy.

13.6 Corridor Seed Consistency

The corridor stage does not start from an arbitrary display snapshot. The code first attempts to use a profile-ready source containing valid corridor vectors; if missing, it falls back to solver snapshots containing knot geometry; if still missing in the RMSE(d) GUI workflow, a technical base snapshot can be rebuilt by local spline refit before launching the grid worker. This base-snapshot hierarchy is scientifically important because corridor width and profile shape depend not only on acceptance thresholds, but also on the basin from which each fixed-$d$ refit is initialized.

13.7 Intentionally Restricted Corridor Refit Budget

Corridor refit budgets are deliberately staged rather than maximal at each point: a first local fixed-$d$ pass and a second polish pass are applied with bounded evaluation counts derived from the corridor refit configuration. This bounded two-pass strategy is designed to keep the corridor as a local profiled sensitivity analysis while still allowing enough relaxation to avoid false failures. In manuscript language, this should be described as a budgeted local profiled refit with robustness fallbacks, not as a full global re-optimization at every tested thickness.

Section 14: UI-to-Engine Bridges (Autoshift, Auto-clean & Dichotomy)

The CERTUS GUI provides direct interaction with the spline algorithms. Key UI features map directly to specific optimization strategies:

14.1 Autoshift and Dichotomous Zoom for Substrate Index Tuning

The Autoshift action performs a four-pass bounded dichotomy search to identify the best substrate index shift. It tests controlled substrate deviations against a local L-BFGS-B polish and selects the shift that minimizes the global RMSE before full free-knot deployment.

14.2 Auto-clean

The Auto-clean button activates a sparsity-enforcing workflow. Rather than running a full $O(N)$ solver pass for each knot, it uses PCHIP interpolation to pre-screen and rank node sensitivity. Only the top candidates are promoted to a full L-BFGS-B polish. It also applies a 2D Neighbor Pull strategy: when a node is removed, the engine tests inward micro-shifts of adjacent nodes to compensate for the lost degree of freedom and reduce artificial RMSE spikes.

14.3 Apply (Polish) and Recall Best RMSE

The Apply (Polish) action triggers a high-fidelity joint optimization. When manual knots are added or deleted, the solver uses PCHIP warm starts. This shape-preserving Hermite interpolant projects the previous $n(\lambda)$ and $k(\lambda)$ onto the new topology, prevents Runge oscillations, and keeps the initial guess within physical bounds:

$$ \min(n_i, n_{i+1}) \le P(x) \le \max(n_i, n_{i+1}) \quad \forall x \in [x_i, x_{i+1}] $$
PCHIP preserves monotonicity and avoids unphysical index excursions during node resampling.

If the optimization degrades due to local minima, the Recall Best RMSE feature restores the best-performing deterministic state.

Section 15: Manuscript-Ready Operational Narrative of the Full Pipeline

The following description is written in a form close to a publishable Methods narrative.

Suggested Methods Narrative
(1) The measured transmission and reflection spectra are sanitized with an objective mask that removes invalid samples and can restrict the fitting window.

(2) The wavelength axis is transformed into reciprocal coordinates $\sigma = 1/\lambda$, and density-corrected quadrature weights are computed on $\ln(\lambda)$ so that sampling density does not bias the objective.

(3) A Swanepoel-based initialization constructs a physically informed warm start for thickness and optical constants, reducing fringe-order aliasing.

(4) A rigid SOL 2 stage estimates thickness and node amplitudes on fixed knot positions to stabilize the global phase structure.

(5) A split free-knot SOL 3 stage releases the geometric degrees of freedom of the refractive and absorptive meshes, enabling local knot concentration near rapidly varying spectral regions.

(6) A transparent-subband refinement (SOL 3b) regularizes low-sensitivity absorptive regions where $\partial T/\partial L \approx 0$, preventing noise-driven zigzag artifacts in $k(\lambda)$.

(7) A spectral mesh polish re-optimizes the final state on a smooth spline representation, improving continuity and reducing residual staircase effects from piecewise-linear intermediate stages.

(8) A joint photometric-scale polish is no longer part of the current pipeline and should be omitted from the method narrative.

(9) Corridor products are generated in two layers: a profile-likelihood acceptance continuation and, when requested, a regular-grid RMSE($d$) diagnostic sweep. The acceptance corridor uses a center best-of-$N$, optional adaptive $\Delta$, parallel or sequential $\pm d$ walks, optional span extension, and boundary bisection. The diagnostic sweep uses grid-centered visit order, breakpoint heuristics, reverse exploration, optional breakpoint-anchored global polish, a P0 neighbour-rescue pass, point provenance, and a base-grid coverage audit. When the local RMSE($d$) valley is sufficiently regular, a local parabolic fit estimates a continuous valley center for display, but the sampled refits remain the primary uncertainty evidence. New absolute-best refits along the diagnostic grid are logged explicitly for traceability.

This sequence is not merely computational convenience. Each phase exists because the full problem is strongly correlated: thickness, refractive dispersion, absorptive structure, and photometric scale can partially compensate each other. CERTUS therefore solves the inverse problem through a controlled release of degrees of freedom, using phase-specific parameterizations to keep the search inside physically interpretable basins.

15.1 Why Phase Ordering Matters Scientifically

The phase order is a key design choice. If all variables were released from the start, the optimizer would face identifiability degeneracies: optical thickness can be preserved by trading $d$ against $n$, transparent-band absorption can oscillate at almost no spectral cost, and local calibration drift can be confused with mild global index reshaping. The staged architecture is therefore better understood as an identifiability management strategy rather than a simple software pipeline.

15.2 What the Returned Solution Actually Represents

The final dictionary returned by the worker is not just a pair of curves. It contains the nominal solution, spectral RMSE diagnostics, best-polished references, the active fitting window metadata, and corridor products when enabled. In publication terms, CERTUS returns both a point estimate and a structured uncertainty object.

15.3 Step-by-Step Reproducibility Checklist for the Article

To make a publication reproducible, the following implementation details should be stated explicitly: the fitted spectral range after masking; whether transmission only or transmission-plus-reflection was optimized; the values of $W_T$ and $W_R$; whether the 12-node or 14-node topology was active; whether monotonic-dispersion penalties were enabled in extraction phases; whether corridor estimation used alpha-threshold or LR-threshold acceptance; for RMSE_ref+$\Delta$ and adaptive-$\Delta$ modes, the values of rmse_abs_tolerance (default $2.5\times 10^{-4}$) and adaptive_rmse_abs_min (default $2.5\times 10^{-5}$) if not left at defaults; whether the reported RMSE(d) comes from continuation acceptance or regular-grid diagnostics; the corridor seed hierarchy; and the regular-grid coverage diagnostics (coverage_complete and fallback counters). These details define the effective inverse problem solved by the code.
