Abstract
CERTUS-FIELD models the electric-field distribution inside multilayer optical coatings under arbitrary angles and polarizations. The module leverages complex admittance recurrences to compute localized physical peak intensities. It integrates a state-of-the-art minimax optimizer that targets Laser-Induced Damage Thresholds (LIDT) to protect optical components from high-power radiation, displaying initial and optimized curves concurrently for detailed screening.

1. Electromagnetic Formulation in Multilayers

When high-power lasers interrogate a dielectric stack, the physical damage mechanism is driven by local peak intensities rather than average spatial energy. Calculating the internal field profile requires solving Maxwell's equations for a plane wave incident at angle $\theta_0$ from a superstrat of index $n_0$ onto a stack of $N$ layers ending in a substrate $n_s$.

Physical Principle: The wave field inside each thin-film layer is represented as a linear combination of right-propagating (forward) and left-propagating (backward) harmonic components. The phase accumulation and interface boundaries are computed using the complex Transfer Matrix Method (TMM).

By enforcing continuity of the tangential electric ($E$) and magnetic ($H$) fields at each interface, the algorithm translates the boundary conditions into a chain of matrix multiplications, mapping the incident wave amplitude to the transmitted state in the substrate.

3. Mathematical Foundations of TMM Field Solver

The core solver operates inside certus_field_core.py. It supports oblique propagation and lossy materials by defining all parameters in the complex plane.

3.1 Snell-Descartes Law and Complex Angles

For an incidence angle $\theta_0$ in the superstrate, the complex angle of refraction $\theta_j$ inside layer $j$ of index $n_j$ is determined by Snell's law: $$ n_0 \sin\theta_0 = n_j \sin\theta_j $$ The complex cosine of the angle is evaluated analytically as: $$ \cos\theta_j = \sqrt{1 - \left(\frac{n_0 \sin\theta_0}{n_j}\right)^2} $$ where the square root branch is selected to ensure a positive real part (passive wave attenuation).

3.2 Polarization Admittances

The electromagnetic boundary conditions differ for transverse electric (TE or S) and transverse magnetic (TM or P) waves. We define the normalized complex optical admittance $Y_j$ of the medium $j$ as: $$ Y_j = \begin{cases} n_j \cos\theta_j & (\text{S-polarization}) \\ \frac{n_j}{\cos\theta_j} & (\text{P-polarization}) \end{cases} $$ Similarly, we compute $Y_0$ for the incident medium and $Y_s$ for the substrate backing.

3.3 Admittance Propagation Recurrence
To compute the reflection coefficient, the algorithm calculates the equivalent input admittance $Y_{\text{eff}}$ of the stack. Starting from the substrate interface with $Y_{\text{eff}}^{(N)} = Y_s$, the equivalent admittance at the input of layer $j$ (thickness $d_j$) is recursively evaluated backwards from layer $N-1$ down to $0$: $$ Y_{\text{eff}}^{(j)} = \frac{Y_{\text{eff}}^{(j+1)} \cos\delta_j + i Y_j \sin\delta_j}{\cos\delta_j + i \left(Y_{\text{eff}}^{(j+1)} / Y_j\right) \sin\delta_j} $$ where the complex phase thickness accumulation is: $$ \delta_j = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda} n_j d_j \cos\theta_j $$ At the entrance interface (superstrate/stack), the amplitude reflection coefficient $r$ and the reflectance $R$ are evaluated as: $$ r = \frac{Y_0 - Y_{\text{eff}}^{(0)}}{Y_0 + Y_{\text{eff}}^{(0)}} \quad ; \quad R = |r|^2 $$

3.4 Forward Field Propagation

Once $r$ is determined, the normalized electric and magnetic field amplitudes at the outer boundary (superstrate interface) are locked as initial values: $$ E_0 = 1 + r \quad ; \quad H_0 = Y_0(1 - r) $$ The tangential fields $\begin{pmatrix} E_j \\ H_j \end{pmatrix}$ at the entrance of layer $j$ are swept forward to the next interface $j+1$ using the inverse characteristic matrix: $$ \begin{pmatrix} E_{j+1} \\ H_{j+1} \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\delta_j & -\frac{i}{Y_j}\sin\delta_j \\ -i Y_j \sin\delta_j & \cos\delta_j \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} E_j \\ H_j \end{pmatrix} $$ This recursive relation maps the fields at all layer interfaces.

3.5 Local Wave Separation and Spatial Field Intensity
Inside any layer $j$, the spatial coordinate $z$ ranges from $0$ (entrance of the layer) to $d_j$ (exit of the layer). The electric field $E_j(z)$ is modeled as the sum of a forward-traveling wave (amplitude $A_j$) and a backward-traveling wave (amplitude $B_j$): $$ E_j(z) = A_j e^{-i \phi_j(z)} + B_j e^{i \phi_j(z)} $$ where the local phase coordinate is $\phi_j(z) = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda} n_j z \cos\theta_j$.

3.5.1 Derivation of Wave Amplitudes

The corresponding magnetic field $H_j(z)$ is related to the electric field by the optical admittance of the layer $Y_j$: $$ H_j(z) = Y_j \left( A_j e^{-i \phi_j(z)} - B_j e^{i \phi_j(z)} \right) $$ By evaluating both equations at the layer entrance ($z = 0$), we obtain the boundary values $E_j$ and $H_j$: $$ E_j(0) = A_j + B_j \quad ; \quad H_j(0) = Y_j(A_j - B_j) $$ Solving this linear system for the complex amplitudes $A_j$ and $B_j$ yields: $$ A_j = \frac{1}{2} \left( E_j + \frac{H_j}{Y_j} \right) \quad ; \quad B_j = \frac{1}{2} \left( E_j - \frac{H_j}{Y_j} \right) $$ These coefficients represent the physical forward and backward waves propagating inside the layer.

3.5.2 Spatial Field Intensity Profile

The normalized local intensity $I_j(z)$ is given by the squared magnitude of the complex electric field: $$ I_j(z) = |E_j(z)|^2 = \left( A_j e^{-i \phi_j(z)} + B_j e^{i \phi_j(z)} \right) \left( A_j^* e^{i \phi_j^*(z)} + B_j^* e^{-i \phi_j^*(z)} \right) $$ For non-absorbing layers (where $n_j$ is purely real and $\phi_j(z)$ is real), this simplifies to: $$ I_j(z) = |A_j|^2 + |B_j|^2 + 2\text{Re}\left( A_j B_j^* e^{-2i \phi_j(z)} \right) $$ Letting $A_j = |A_j| e^{i \phi_A}$ and $B_j = |B_j| e^{i \phi_B}$, we can express the interference term explicitly: $$ I_j(z) = |A_j|^2 + |B_j|^2 + 2 |A_j| |B_j| \cos\left( \phi_A - \phi_B - 2\phi_j(z) \right) $$

3.5.3 Analytical Peak Finding (Hotspot Localization)

Since the cosine term is bounded by $1$, the absolute maximum intensity that can physically exist in the material is: $$ I_{\max, j} = (|A_j| + |B_j|)^2 $$ This physical upper bound is reached if and only if there is a coordinate $z \in [0, d_j]$ where the waves interfere constructively (i.e. the cosine argument is a multiple of $2\pi$): $$ \phi_A - \phi_B - 2\phi_j(z) = 2k\pi \quad (k \in \mathbb{Z}) $$ $$ 2\phi_j(z) = (\phi_A - \phi_B) - 2k\pi $$ Since the coordinate $z$ is bounded by the layer boundaries $[0, d_j]$, the phase coordinate $2\phi_j(z)$ is constrained to the interval $[0, 2\text{Re}(\delta_j)]$, where $\delta_j = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda} n_j d_j \cos\theta_j$ is the total phase thickness of the layer. This requirement is evaluated numerically using: $$ \phi_{\text{diff}} = (\arg A_j - \arg B_j) \bmod 2\pi \le 2 \text{Re}(\delta_j) $$
  • If the condition is met: The constructive interference peak falls inside the physical layer boundaries, so: $$ |E_{\max, j}|^2 = (|A_j| + |B_j|)^2 $$
  • If the condition is not met: The intensity function is strictly monotonic inside the layer, meaning the peak is located at one of the interfaces: $$ |E_{\max, j}|^2 = \max\left( I_j(0), \,\, I_j(d_j) \right) $$
In summary, the exact spatial peak intensity for a non-absorbing layer is: $$ |E_{\max, j}|^2 = \max\left( I_j(0), \,\, I_j(d_j), \,\, (|A_j| + |B_j|)^2 \text{ if } \phi_{\text{diff}} \le 2 \text{Re}(\delta_j) \right) $$ If the layer has absorption, the phase thickness is complex, and the solver falls back to a fine grid evaluation of $I_j(z)$ to guarantee accuracy.

3.5.4 Integrated and Average Descriptors

  • Integrated Intensity: $\int_0^{d_j} I_j(z) dz \approx \sum_{k} \frac{I_j(z_k) + I_j(z_{k+1})}{2} (z_{k+1} - z_k)$ (Trapezoidal Rule over integral_points grid)
  • Average Intensity: $\bar{I}_j = \frac{1}{d_j} \int_0^{d_j} I_j(z) dz$

4.1 Multi-Start L-BFGS-B (Local Refinement)

The local optimization engine uses the L-BFGS-B algorithm, a quasi-Newton solver designed to handle box-constrained parameters. Since thin-film optical objectives are highly non-linear with many local minima, a single local minimization can easily get trapped. To address this, the solver executes a Multi-Start routine:

  1. Nominal Run: Enters optimization using the current design thicknesses as the starting vector $x_0$.
  2. Perturbed Runs (2x): Generates two additional starting points by applying a random uniform scaling factor between $0.85$ and $1.15$ to each layer thickness.
  3. Selection: Minimizes each start point separately. Once all runs finish, it ranks the solutions by cost and returns the absolute best configuration.

4.2 PGLOBAL (Global Search)

When the Global Optimization mode is enabled, the worker switches to the premium PGLOBAL algorithm, the exact same multi-start stochastic global solver used in the design module:

  • Sobol Quasi-Random Adaptive Sampling: Draws highly balanced, low-discrepancy candidate points across the thickness boundaries using scrambled Sobol sequences.
  • Batch Evaluation & Reduction: Evaluates candidate points in parallel and keeps the top fraction (e.g., 20% to 30%) by sorting and partitioning their objective values.
  • Single-Linkage Clustering (MLSL): Performs distance-based clustering on the unit hypercube to group candidates near known minima, identifying unique search basins and preventing redundant local searches.
  • Parallel Local Sweeps: Launches box-constrained local refinement runs (L-BFGS-B) from each unclustered candidate seed in parallel to converge precisely to the best global optimum.

4.3 Monte-Carlo Tolerancing

To assess the manufacturing robustness of the designs, the module implements a Monte-Carlo simulation:

  • Gaussian Thickness Perturbation: Random noise is added to the nominal thicknesses: $d_j \sim \mathcal{N}(d_j^{(0)}, \,\, (d_j^{(0)} \cdot \sigma)^2)$ where $\sigma$ is the user-configured error percentage.
  • Statistical Ensemble: Runs the wave solver over multiple iterations (e.g. 50 or 100 runs) with perturbed stacks.
  • Hotspot and Spectral Variance: Computes the envelope of the electric field intensity profiles and spectral variations, showing how the physical damage thresholds are affected by layer thickness errors.

5. LIDT-Weighted Optimization Mathematics

In high-power laser systems, the Laser-Induced Damage Threshold (LIDT) defines the maximum energy density a material can withstand. In multi-layer stacks, H-index and L-index layers possess different mechanical and chemical bonding strengths, resulting in distinct threshold values $LIDT_H$ and $LIDT_L$ (represented by the user controls seuil_int_1 and seuil_int_2).

The total cost function is the sum of a reflectance constraint term $Cost_R$ and a field penalty term. The reflectance constraint is defined as: $$ Cost_R = \begin{cases} 100 \cdot (R_{\min} - R)^2 & \text{if } R < R_{\min} \\ 100 \cdot (R - R_{\max})^2 & \text{if } R > R_{\max} \\ 0 & \text{if } R_{\min} \le R \le R_{\max} \end{cases} $$ where $R$ is the calculated reflectance. If no custom constraints are active (i.e. $R_{\min}=1$ and $R_{\max}=1$), the code defaults to minimizing the transmission loss: $Cost_R = 1 - R$.

5.1 Standard Barrier Mode

In the default configuration (min_field_active = False), the optimization objective function applies a quadratic penalty barrier only when worst-case layer intensities exceed their respective thresholds: $$ Cost = Cost_R + \alpha \cdot \left[ \max\left(0, \,\, \frac{|E_{\max, H}|^2}{LIDT_H} - 1\right) \right]^2 + \alpha \cdot \left[ \max\left(0, \,\, \frac{|E_{\max, L}|^2}{LIDT_L} - 1\right) \right]^2 $$ where $|E_{\max, H}|^2$ and $|E_{\max, L}|^2$ represent the absolute peak intensities in the H and L layers. While this discourages catastrophic configurations, the field penalty term falls to zero once the fields are below the thresholds, leaving the optimizer to only optimize reflectance.

5.2 Active Minimax Minimization Mode

When the Active Field Minimization checkbox is enabled (min_field_active = True), the optimizer switches to a continuous minimax objective. The cost function is defined as: $$ Cost = Cost_R + \alpha \cdot \max\left( \frac{|E_{\max, H}|^2}{LIDT_H}, \,\, \frac{|E_{\max, L}|^2}{LIDT_L} \right) $$ where:

  • $|E_{\max, H}|^2$ and $|E_{\max, L}|^2$ are the absolute worst-case local peak intensities across all H and L layers respectively.
  • $LIDT_H$ and $LIDT_L$ are the physical damage thresholds.
  • $\alpha$ (alpha) represents the active field weighting coefficient.

Key Consequence: Because the electric field inside a dielectric structure is always positive, the minimax cost term is strictly greater than zero. The optimization algorithm actively and continuously reshapes the layer thicknesses to minimize field hotspots, even when the fields are well below nominal damage thresholds.

6. Visual Overlay and Comparison

To help engineers evaluate the optimization trade-offs, the module implements a dual-curve comparison system:

  • Initial State (Dashed Lines): The pre-optimized curves ($|E|^2$ profile and spectral reflectance $R(\lambda)$) are cached when optimization is triggered. They are rendered as thin dashed lines (style Qt.PenStyle.DashLine) for direct visual screening.
  • Optimized State (Solid Lines): The active designs are rendered as thick solid lines, allowing immediate validation of the optimized field compression.
This visual comparison helps engineers confirm that field hotspots have been reduced without compromising spectral performance.

It enables engineers to optimize both spectral responses and spatial field profiles to prevent laser-induced damage.
