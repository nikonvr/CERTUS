CERTUS-METROLOGY

Metrology Protocol for High-Precision Optical Constants (n, k)

Differential Measurement & Uncertainty Budget

1. Prior determination of substrate index ($n_{s}$): choosing transmission vs. reflection

The envelope method relies on accurate knowledge of the bare-substrate refractive index ($n_{s}$). Experimentally it can be obtained from transmission ($T_{sub}$) or reflection ($R_{sub}$). Each path has a distinct trade-off against instrumental (non-linearity) and physical (absorption) limits.

1.1. Transmission (T): photometric linearity and vulnerability

In a strictly transparent region ($k = 0$), with incoherent multiple reflections between front and back faces of a double-sided sapphire slab, transmission is:

$$ T_{sub} = \frac{2 n_{s}}{n_{s}^2 + 1} $$

Inverting yields the substrate index:

$$ n_{s} = \frac{1}{T_{sub}} + \sqrt{\frac{1}{T_{sub}^2} - 1} $$

Advantage of transmission (linearity): measuring Tsub compares the reference beam (100% in air) to a signal attenuated mainly by Fresnel reflections (~86% for sapphire). The relatively small dynamic range keeps the detector in a more linear regime, limiting raw photometric error (ΔTsub).

Pitfall — mathematical amplification: even when ΔTsub is small, inversion amplifies it. From the differential of (1), propagated error is:

Δns = [ (ns2 + 1)2 / (2|ns2 - 1|) ] × ΔTsub(3)

For sapphire the bracketed factor ranges from 3.8 to 4.4. Using realistic non-linearity specs (see final budget, Section 7):

  • 400 nm (UV): ns ≈ 1.78. Detector very linear, ΔTsub = 0.10% (0.001).
    Uncertainty: Δns = 3.8 × 0.001 = ± 0.0038.
  • 1000 nm (NIR): ns ≈ 1.75. ΔTsub = 0.10%.
    Uncertainty: Δns = 4.0 × 0.001 = ± 0.0040.
  • 4500 nm (MIR): ns ≈ 1.67. Lower SNR, ΔTsub = 0.20%.
    Uncertainty: Δns = 4.4 × 0.002 = ± 0.0088.

Physical drawback (absorption trap): inversion assumes perfect transparency. Residual absorption (α > 0, UV/IR tails) lowers Tsub; the model may misinterpret this as higher reflectance and overestimate ns.

1.2. Reflection (R): robustness to absorption and instrumental penalty

To bound the transparent window and extract the real part of n without absorption bias, double-sided reflection (Rsub) is recommended.

Physical advantage: first-order analysis shows R is structurally less sensitive to bulk absorption than T. With internal attenuation x = e-αd ≈ 1 - αd and per-face Fresnel reflectance R0:

T ≈ Tideal (1 - αd)
R ≈ R0 + R0(1 - R0)2 (1 - 2αd)

Sensitivity ratio for sapphire (ns ≈ 1.76, R0 ≈ 7.6%, Tideal ≈ 86%):

| ΔRabs | / | ΔTabs | = [ 2 R0 (1 - R0)2 ] / Tideal ≈ 0.15

Reflection is roughly 7× less sensitive to internal absorption because the dominant reflected path does not traverse the bulk.

Instrumental drawback: Rsub is small (~14%). Comparing to a near-100% reference drives the detector into a more non-linear regime; ΔR is often 2–3× ΔT in practice.

Metrology summary: use R to flag absorption bands and secure n at spectral edges; use T in the strictly transparent window for better linearity.

2. Experimental context and acquisition strategy

The goal is residual absorption (k < 10-3) and refractive index n of dielectric films on sapphire (Al2O3). Following H.A. Macleod, thick films (~3 μm) make weak absorption visible in the spectrum.

2.1. Deposition: Helios 800 (PARMS)

Films are produced by plasma-assisted reactive magnetron sputtering (PARMS) on Helios 800 equipment. Compared with classic e-beam evaporation (low adatom energy, columnar porosity), PARMS ion bombardment (10–100 eV) increases packing density toward unity and reduces index inhomogeneity even at 3 μm, consistent with P.J. Martin and Macleod on ion assist.

This structural stability supports ideal Swanepoel envelope formulas and reduces envelope “pinching” from index gradients (Section 8.1).

2.2. Spectral ranges and adaptive resolution

Spectra are acquired on a Photon RT 5200. Analysis relies on Fabry–Pérot fringe visibility:

  • SiO2: 250–5200 nm (transparent over the band).
  • Nb2O5: 350–5200 nm (below 350 nm interband absorption removes fringe contrast).

UV–vis: fine sampling (<1 nm) and small spot. IR: coarser resolution and larger spot for SNR where fringes are sparse.

2.3. Relative measurement: compensating double-sided substrate absorption

Major error source: sapphire absorption (UV/IR tails). Use differential transmission ratio Rraw = Tsample / Tbare.

On double-sided polished substrates, Tbare already includes back-side Fresnel and bulk attenuation, so the ratio largely cancels sapphire absorption. Moderate index (~7.6% per interface) keeps second-order errors from the coated front small.

3. Swanepoel envelope formalism

Envelopes are defined by optical phase (Swanepoel):

  • Half-wave envelope (T1/2): optical thickness multiple of λ/2; film is “optically absent”. Layer absorption is read on this envelope.
  • Quarter-wave envelope (T1/4): odd multiples of λ/4; maximum interference contrast.

Relative to sapphire (ns ≈ 1.76):

  • Nb2O5 (n > ns): quarter-wave raises reflectance → T1/4 < T1/2.
  • SiO2 (n < ns): quarter-wave acts as AR → T1/4 > T1/2.

4. Rigorous algebraic extraction

Work with envelope ratios of r = T/Tbare: rM (upper), rm (lower). Bare-substrate transmission:

Tbare(λ) = 2 ns / (ns2 + 1)(1)

Intermediate quantity and film index:

N = (ns2 + 1)/2 × [ 1 + 2(rM - rm)/(rMrm) ](2)
n(λ) = [ N + (N2 - ns2)1/2 ]1/2(3)

For extinction, use upper absolute envelope TM = rM Tbare:

EM = (8 n2 ns) / (rMTbare) + (n2 - 1)(n2 - ns2)(4)
x = [ EM - (EM2 - (n2-1)3(n2-ns4))1/2 ] / [ (n-1)3(n-ns2) ](5)

Both roots are evaluated; the physical root satisfies 0 < x < 1.

k(λ) = - [ λ / (4π d) ] ln(x)(6)

5. Analytical uncertainty propagation (full case)

5.1. Photometric non-linearity and ns

ΔT depends on detector non-linearity; it is smallest (~0.05%) when measured flux is near reference (~100%).

ns is derived from bare sapphire Tn ≈ 86%, so ΔTn ≈ 0.1–0.2%. Propagation:

Δns = [ (ns2 + 1)2 / (2|ns2 - 1|) ] × ΔTn(7)

5.2. Film index uncertainty Δn

With contrast C = | 1/T1/4 - 1/T1/2 |:

ΔN = (2ns / T1/42)ΔT1/4 + (2ns / T1/22)ΔT1/2 + ( ns + 2C ) Δns(8)
Δn = [ 1 / | n - ns2/n3 | ] × [ ΔN + (ns / n2) Δns ](9)

5.3. Extinction coefficient Δk

Let A = (n2-1)3(n2-ns4), B = (n-1)3(n-ns2).

ΔA = | 6n(n2-1)2(n2-ns4) + 2n(n2-1)3 | Δn + | 4ns3(n2-1)3 | Δns(10)
ΔB = | 3(n-1)2(n-ns2) + (n-1)3 | Δn + | 2ns(n-1)3 | Δns(11)
ΔE = [ 8n2ns / T1/22 ] ΔT1/2 + | 16n ns / T1/2 + 2n(n2-ns2) + 2n(n2-1) | Δn + | 8n2 / T1/2 - 2ns(n2-1) | Δns(12)

With S = (E2 - A)1/2:

Δx = (1 / |B|) × [ ΔE + (1/S)(|E|ΔE + 0.5ΔA) + x ΔB ](13)
Δk = [ λ / (4π d) ] × ( Δx / x ) + k × ( Δd / d )(14)

6. Relative vs. absolute measurement

Absolute measurement (sample vs. air, no bare reference) is compared to the differential ratio method.

6.1. Swanepoel in absolute mode

Treating substrate attenuation as a simple Beer–Lambert factor is not rigorous. In Swanepoel’s stack model the substrate is an active element: internal transmittance xs couples with incoherent multiple reflections between film/substrate and substrate/air. Uncertainty Δxs propagates nonlinearly into layer x.

8. Method limits and experimental artifacts

Algebra uses ideal Swanepoel formulas; Macleod notes that thick films expose measurements to artifacts that distort T1/4 and T1/2.

8.1. Perfect homogeneity assumption

PVD often yields columnar growth and index gradients; inhomogeneity pinches fringes and can mimic higher k.

8.2. Thickness non-uniformity over the beam

Small Δd across the spot dephases rays and smooths fringes, appearing as spurious k.

8.3. Spectral resolution

At 3 μm and short λ, fringe spacing can be a few nm; finite bandwidth averages fringes and looks like absorption.

8.4. Surface scattering

Loss on T1/2 is attributed to absorption; rough thick films scatter light out of the detector acceptance, inflating apparent k.

9. Global numerical refinement on continuous spectrum

Envelope algebra is discrete at extrema. For smooth dispersion and noise averaging, a global optimization step is used.

9.1. Envelopes as initial guess (seed)

Thick-film spectra have many fringes; random starts trap in wrong interference orders. Discrete n(λ), k(λ), d from envelopes provide a physically anchored starting point.

9.2. B-splines and RMSE on differential ratio

Dispersion is parameterized with B-splines for n and k. The merit function must not fit absolute sample transmission (Section 6). Minimize RMSE between theoretical Tcalc,sample/Tcalc,bare and measured Rraw over all wavelengths.

RMSE = [ (1/N) ∑ ( Ratiocalc - Rraw )2 ]1/2

9.3. Phase coupling and constrained relaxation

Fixing ns with small systematic error forces non-physical twists in n(λ) over ~100 fringes. Use bounded relaxation: film via free B-splines and ±2% on d; substrate via rigid Sellmeier plus vertical offset Δns capped at ±0.005 (Section 1). Thick Fabry–Pérot fringes can self-calibrate ns better than bare-substrate measurement alone.

9.4. Global fit benefits

  • Statistical averaging over thousands of spectral points.
  • Fine d adjustment for fringe alignment.
  • Smooth n,k from B-splines with physical Sellmeier substrate.
